Udi Nir makes documentary films together with his partner and creative collaborator, Sagi Bornstein – a long-standing partnership driven by a fascination with larger-than-life characters and the possibility of dismantling and reassembling reality through found footage. “We try to tell important stories through interesting means,” he says. The turning point came over a decade ago, in a chance encounter with producer Georg Tschurtschenthaler, who believed in them and in a peculiar idea: a documentary about the Israeli youth delegations to Poland, where teenagers learn about the Holocaust – a film made entirely from YouTube videos. The result, #Uploading_Holocaust (Docaviv 2017), marked the beginning of a distinctive cinematic language – one that uses found footage to reexamine collective memory and familiar narratives. Since then, they have made Golda, Viral, We Must Talk Now, and The First Lady – films that have received international recognition, been distributed in dozens of countries, and won awards, including the IDA Award, the Japan Prize, and the Docaviv Editing Award. Working with found footage, particularly from everyday platforms like YouTube, allows them to create films that are unique in form and narrative. Alongside his filmmaking, Nir is active in the local documentary community as a board member of the Israeli Documentary Forum and produces films by other filmmakers. He is currently producing two feature-length films and directing other films. He draws inspiration from films that have stayed with him long after he had watched them, such as The War of Raya Sinitsina and The War – examples, in his opinion, of documentary cinema’s power to offer empathy and hold complexity without simplifying it.
**Personal message**  
“I don’t like the word ‘message’ because it doesn’t leave much room for listening and complexity. But in recent years, and especially under the current government, our society has hit a moral low point that obliges us – especially us documentary filmmakers – to look directly at the system we are all part of and illuminate the corners it is trying to keep dark: from the destruction of Gaza, through the pogroms in the West Bank, to the abandonment of Arab society in Israel and the daily threat to the few democratic mechanisms this country still has.”

For Shauly Melamed, cinema is an obsession. “I still haven’t managed to figure out where the line between cinema and life is,” he says.
